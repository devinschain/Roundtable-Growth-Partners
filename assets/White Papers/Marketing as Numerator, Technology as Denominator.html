Marketing as Numerator, Technology as Denominator

Every company can now buy the same AI. Growth will go to the ones that know something the others don't.

Roundtable Growth Partners  ·  November 2026

We are spending on AI. Where is the growth?

Every leadership team is asking this question. The spending is real: Gartner's 2026 survey finds CMOs now put an average of 15.3% of marketing budgets into AI. The readiness is not: only 30% say they are mature.

15.3%of marketing budgets now go to AI, on average
30%of CMOs report mature AI readiness

Most teams use AI to do the same work cheaper. Cheaper is something every competitor can buy on the same day. To see why that matters, write growth as a ratio.

Growth efficiency =
Marketingcustomers won × value per customer
Technology and ITcost, risk and delay per customer

AI is collapsing the bottom of that fraction for everyone at once. That leaves one place to build an advantage: the top.

The denominator is becoming a commodity

AI cuts the cost per decision, per asset and per follow-up, and it keeps getting better and cheaper for every company. That is what happens when a denominator matures. It stops being a differentiator and becomes table stakes.

Klarna is a clean example. It used generative AI for images and copy, cut agency spend by 25%, and shortened image cycles from six weeks to seven days. Those are real savings, and the company reported them without any conversion data alongside. Any competitor can run the same playbook next quarter.

So the numerator has to be what others cannot copy. Two things qualify.

What a brand knows about itself

  • Why customers actually choose you, in their own words
  • Results of past tests, including the failures
  • Margin by segment, product and channel
  • Sales objections and how they were overcome
  • The voice, standards and promises that define the brand

The judgment applied to it

  • Which questions are worth testing
  • Whether a result reflects long-term value or a quick click
  • When to scale an offer and when to kill it
  • Where the risk is too high for an agent to act alone

One caution keeps this honest. Judge the numerator on outcomes. Netflix's own researchers warn that click-through and engagement may not reflect long-term value, so track retention and margin, not only clicks.

The 1990s version ran with no AI at all

None of this is new. Capital One ran the same loop thirty years ago, and its results show what a learning loop is worth. Its founders put the idea in one line.

Credit cards aren't banking, they're information.

Richard Fairbank, CEO, Capital One
36,000tests run in 1999 alone
55%+of the $750M marketing budget went to products under six months old
1,000%+stock gain from the 1994 IPO to 2000, against just under 300% for the S&P 500

The test behind the numbers

When a customer called to say "I'm leaving," Capital One did not guess who was bluffing. It split those callers at random into three groups. One had the bluff called and the account closed. One got the competing offer matched. One was met halfway. It tracked what each group did next, built a model, and now the representative sees an instant recommendation on screen. That is a pilot with a holdout group and a decision rule, run long before anyone used those words.

What the later chapters add

  • Controls belong in the denominator. A misconfigured application exposed data on about 106 million people in 2019, and regulators fined the bank $80M. Cost per customer includes the cost of a breach.
  • Check the agents. Capital One's multi-agent assistant for auto dealers reports up to 55% gains in lead conversion (company-reported), and includes an evaluator agent that checks every plan against policy before it runs.
  • Buy speed, own learning. The $5.15B Brex purchase in April 2026 bought an AI-native platform. The data and underwriting stayed its own.

Swap underwriting for technology and the story reads as if it were written for today. The better your marketing, the better your technology. The better your technology, the better your marketing.

Who owns the ratio

Every client needs one person or team inside the company who answers for both marketing and technology. Without that owner, the loop stops when the outside adviser leaves.

Capital One's founders learned this the hard way. They began as consultants, but the bank that hired them judged the idea too radical for outsiders to carry out. They had to join full time, and they made their acceptance conditional on controlling strategy, marketing and IT, with technologists working beside analysts and marketers under one leader.

For firms that work from the outside, the equivalent is an owner on the client side with four responsibilities:

  1. The shared scorecard. One ratio that neither marketing nor IT can hide behind.
  2. The test register. Every test logged with its decision rule and result, failures included. Booking.com keeps exactly this record, and runs over 1,000 experiments at once.
  3. The data foundation. One customer record that marketing, sales and technology all trust.
  4. Governance. Rules for access, review and audit for any AI agent that touches the CRM.

The outside partner designs the loop and trains the team. The inside owner keeps it running.

Seven rules and a first pilot

  1. Build the data foundation before scaling AI.
  2. Keep one customer record, so marketing sees what sales closed and technology sees what marketing promised.
  3. Make every campaign a test with one question, one owner and a decision rule set before launch.
  4. Judge results on outcomes, not proxies.
  5. Put an independent check around AI agents, with human review before an agent acts.
  6. Treat governance as part of the denominator, because weak controls set the cost of failure.
  7. Write kill criteria and reallocate. Capital One withdrew its famous balance transfer product in 1994 as returns fell, while rivals kept pushing it and some, like Advanta, blew up.

Run one pilot

Scale is not the point. Start small, with the same discipline.

  • Baseline one client's ratio with the six numbers above
  • Name the inside owner for both marketing and technology
  • Write one test question, a decision rule and kill criteria before launch
  • Set up a holdout group that does not receive the change
  • Log the result, good or bad, in a shared test register
  • Draft a short governance checklist for any AI agent in the CRM

A note on the analogy

The link between marketing, technology and Capital One's underwriting is our analogy, not Capital One's own claim. Its scale of 36,000 tests a year is not a client's scale, so pilot smaller with the same discipline. Several figures are company-reported, and Capital One's record after 2000 includes a serious breach and integration that ran slower than announced.
