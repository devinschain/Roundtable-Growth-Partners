Marketing as Numerator, Technology as Denominator

Improve both sides of the ratio, and let each feed the other.

Roundtable Growth Partners  ·  November 2026

Executive summary

Growth efficiency is a ratio, and most companies work on only half of it. Marketing is the numerator: customers won times the value of each. Technology is the denominator: the cost, risk and delay carried by each customer won.

Growth efficiency =
Marketingcustomers won × value per customer
Technology and ITcost, risk and delay per customer

Capital One showed in the 1990s that when marketing and technology run as one learning loop, the ratio improves from both sides. AI now lets any growth team run a version of that loop. But AI gets cheaper and better for everyone, so it changes where the edge sits.

Credit cards aren't banking, they're information.

Richard Fairbank, CEO, Capital One

Swap underwriting for technology and the story reads as if it were written for today. The better your marketing, the better your technology. The better your technology, the better your marketing.

Three ideas carry this paper:

  • Improve both sides together. A better denominator lets marketing target and price more sharply, and better marketing gives technology better data to learn from.
  • Let AI lower the denominator, and protect the numerator. The numerator is what competitors cannot copy: what a brand knows about itself, and the human judgment applied to it.
  • Put one owner inside every client. One person or team should answer for both marketing and technology, so the loop keeps running after outside advisers leave.

The ratio: marketing over technology

The ratio improves from either side, but the edge comes from improving both at once. Each side sets the limits of the other.

Marketing (numerator)Technology and IT (denominator)
What it measuresCustomers won, pipeline, revenue, value per customerCost, risk and delay per customer won
What it holdsBrand, offers, targeting, content, sales follow-upData, CRM, automation, AI, security, controls
How it improvesSharper targeting, faster test cycles, better offersCheaper decisions, faster delivery, fewer failures
What it gives the other sideFresh first-party data to learn fromA system that turns data into decisions

Most firms treat these as two budgets run by two teams. Marketing buys campaigns that technology cannot measure. Technology builds systems that marketing cannot use. The ratio stays flat because neither side feeds the other.

The denominator is also more than a cost line. Better data and tooling let marketing aim and price more precisely. That is why the ratio improves from both sides at once, and why splitting the two teams hides the gain.

What Capital One got right

Capital One treated every offer as an experiment and built its organization so technology, analytics and marketing served one objective. Its founders put it simply: credit cards are not banking, they are information. By 1999 the company ran about 36,000 tests a year, and its stock had risen more than 1,000% since its 1994 IPO, against just under 300% for the S&P 500 (Harvard Business School case 9-700-124).

Six mechanics from that case carry straight into a marketing and technology model.

Capital One by the numbers

Four figures from the case show the loop at work.

A test in one paragraph

When a customer called Capital One to say "I'm leaving," the company did not guess who was bluffing. It split those callers at random into three groups. Group one had the bluff called and the account closed. Group two got the competing offer matched. Group three was met halfway. It then tracked what each group did next and built a model from the results. Today the service representative sees an instant recommendation on screen, such as lowering the rate to 12.9%.

That is a pilot with a holdout group and a decision rule, run long before anyone used those words. It is the smallest unit of the loop: one question, randomized groups, and one result that feeds the next decision.

What the habit bought

The habit showed up in the budget. Of its $750 million marketing spend in 1999, more than 55% went to products that did not exist six months earlier. It also treated data as the asset. Before Fairbank and Morris arrived, the bank that hired them routinely erased and reused customer data tapes to save $40 each.

The pattern is a loop, not a tool. Marketing creates data, technology turns it into decisions, and the decisions sharpen the next round of marketing.

What changed with AI

AI lowers the denominator and raises the numerator at the same time. It cuts the cost per decision, per asset and per follow-up, and it sharpens targeting and shortens test cycles. The catch is that it does this for every competitor. A cost advantage that anyone can rent is not an edge for long.

Capital One's record since 2000 shows what lasts. The loop survived, but the denominator was rebuilt three times: first owned data, then an owned cloud platform, then an owned AI stack.

The lesson for clients is to own the parts of the denominator that generate learning, such as the data and the models, and to rent or buy the rest. Most teams have not built that system yet. Gartner's 2026 survey found CMOs put an average of 15.3% of marketing budgets into AI, while only 30% report mature AI readiness.

What cannot be copied

If AI belongs in the denominator, the numerator has to be what others cannot copy. Two things qualify: what a brand knows about itself, and the human judgment applied to that knowledge.

What a brand knows about itself

  • Why customers actually choose you, in their own words
  • Results of past tests, including the failures
  • Margin by segment, product and channel
  • Sales objections and how they were overcome
  • The voice, standards and promises that define the brand

Human judgment

  • Which questions are worth testing
  • Whether a result reflects long-term value or only a quick click
  • When to scale an offer and when to kill it
  • Where the risk is too high for an agent to act alone

Two cautions keep this honest. Cost savings alone do not prove the numerator improved: a company can cut agency spend by a quarter and still have no conversion data to show for it. And proxies mislead. Click-through and engagement may not track retention or margin, so the scorecard should measure outcomes.

This is why a shared test register matters. A running record of every test, the decision rule set beforehand, and the result, failures included, turns one-off experiments into knowledge a competitor cannot rent.

The inside owner

Every client needs one person or team inside the company who answers for both marketing and technology. Without that owner, the loop stops when the outside adviser leaves.

The Capital One story makes the point. Its founders began as consultants, but the bank that hired them judged the idea too radical for outsiders to carry out. They had to join full time, and they made their acceptance conditional on controlling strategy, marketing and IT, with technologists working beside analysts and marketers under one leader.

For firms that work from the outside, the equivalent is an owner on the client side with four responsibilities:

  1. The shared scorecard. One ratio, such as pipeline value per dollar of cost to serve, that neither marketing nor IT can hide behind.
  2. The test register. Every test logged with its decision rule and result, failures included.
  3. The data foundation. One customer record that marketing, sales and technology all trust.
  4. Governance. Rules for access, review and audit for any AI agent that touches the CRM.

The outside partner designs the loop and trains the team. The inside owner keeps it running.

Seven practices and a pilot checklist

  1. Build the data foundation before scaling AI. Data, governance and talent come first. Gartner's 2026 survey finds only 30% of marketing leaders report mature AI readiness.
  2. Keep one customer record, so marketing sees what sales closed and technology sees what marketing promised.
  3. Make every campaign a test with one question, one owner and a decision rule set before launch. Booking.com runs over 1,000 experiments at once and keeps a searchable record of every win and failure.
  4. Judge results on outcomes, not proxies. Track retention and margin, not only clicks. Netflix's own researchers warn that click-through and engagement may not reflect long-term value.
  5. Put an independent check around AI agents, with human review before an agent acts. Capital One's multi-agent assistant for auto dealers includes an evaluator agent that checks every plan against policy before it runs.
  6. Treat governance as part of the denominator, because weak controls set the cost of failure.
  7. Write kill criteria and reallocate. Retire what stops working. Capital One withdrew its famous balance transfer product in 1994 as returns fell, while rivals kept pushing it and some, like Advanta, blew up.

Run one pilot

Capital One's scale is not a client's scale, so start small with the same discipline.

  • Baseline one client's ratio: pipeline value over cost and delay per customer won
  • Name the inside owner for both marketing and technology
  • Write one test question, a decision rule and kill criteria before launch
  • Set up a holdout group that does not receive the change
  • Log the result, good or bad, in a shared test register
  • Draft a short governance checklist for any AI agent in the CRM

How this applies to Roundtable

Roundtable's capabilities already match the loop. Each needs one upgrade, and the positioning becomes explicit: marketing is the numerator, technology is the denominator, and we improve both together.

The existing results point the same way: 125% more leads and a 42% ROAS improvement for ZMC & Associates, and 3× faster follow-up for the sales team of a $300M distributor.

A note on the analogy

The link between marketing, technology and Capital One's underwriting is our analogy, not Capital One's own claim. Capital One's scale of 36,000 tests a year is not a client's scale, so pilot smaller with the same discipline. Several figures are company-reported, and Capital One's record after 2000 includes a serious breach and integration that ran slower than announced.
