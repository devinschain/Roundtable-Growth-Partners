Section 1

What is Roundtable Growth Partners?

Roundtable Growth Partners (RGP) is a focused growth operator. We sit alongside founders and operators at the moment they are ready to move from one revenue plateau to the next, and we stay until the system that drives that growth is theirs to run.

We are not a strategy consultancy. We do not hand over a deck. We build the funnel, score the partners, model the tuck-in, and run the first tests alongside your team. The growth system becomes embedded in the business, not dependent on us.

We know not every idea works, and that is the point. We prove what does before you commit significant people or capital.

The problem we solve

Most growth stalls for one of three reasons.

1

There is no shared language for what is actually working, so traction runs on instinct, not evidence.

2

Opportunities are evaluated on attractiveness, not sequence, so the flashy option crowds out the right next step.

3

The person accountable for growth is also accountable for everything else.

RGP addresses all three. We bring structure, sequencing, and a single accountable operator.

What we are not

  • A marketing agency. We do not run ads on retainer.
  • An M&A advisor. We do not earn on transaction close.
  • A fractional CMO service. We own outcomes, not a job title.
  • A software product. We are operators, not a platform.

Pillar 1 · Testing

Know before you grow.

The most common growth mistake is calling something a test when it is really a launch. A test earns the right to scale. A pilot tells you where to look next. These are different things, and they require different setups.

Test vs. pilot: the core distinction

TestPilot
PurposeIsolate one variable at real volumeDirectional signal; tells you where to look
VolumeStatistically meaningfulSmall; the intent is learning, not proof
OutputGo / no-go decision to scaleHypothesis for the next test
RiskSpend is real; the result is definitiveLow spend; the result is provisional
Temptation to avoidCalling a pilot a testCalling a test a pilot to avoid committing

The testing process

  1. Define the growth question: what decision will this test inform?
  2. Identify the one variable being isolated: channel, offer, price, message, or audience.
  3. Set the success condition before launch: what result earns the right to scale?
  4. Determine the minimum volume for statistical confidence.
  5. Run the test without touching the variable mid-flight.
  6. Read the result against the pre-set condition, not against hope.
  7. Document and share: what worked, what did not, and what the next test should be.

Common failure modes

  • Changing the variable mid-test because early results are disappointing.
  • Running at too low a volume, then treating the output as proof.
  • Testing multiple variables at once and attributing outcomes to the wrong one.
  • Skipping the success condition and deciding after the fact.

A test that cannot be read is a spend, not a test. If you cannot define success before you start, you are not ready to test.

Pillar 2 · Partnerships

Double down on what you do best.

A partnership is a distribution agreement. The goal is to reach a qualified audience through a trusted intermediary at unit economics that make the channel worth keeping. Almost everything else is noise.

Partnerships are not an expense. Chosen on evidence rather than enthusiasm, they are an inexpensive way to create value together. The most common mistake is leading with relationships rather than rubric, pursuing the most visible or enthusiastic partner rather than the sequentially right one.

The four affinity models

Before ranking candidates, map the universe of partnership types by how they work.

Affinity marketingThe partner's audience has a shared identity or interest that aligns with your offer: associations, alumni networks, professional groups.
Co-marketingJoint campaigns where both parties share a customer base and promote each other.
Embedded distributionYour product or offer is integrated into a partner's existing purchase or membership flow.
White-labelThe partner sells your product under their brand; you provide fulfillment and economics.

Partner scoring rubric

The sequencing principle

The right first partner is not the most attractive one. It is the one that, if it works, makes the next conversation easier.

Ask: what does a successful test with this partner prove, and who does that proof open the door to?

  • Start with the partner where the test is cleanest and the economics are most readable.
  • Build the case study before you need it in the next negotiation.
  • Do not sign an exclusive arrangement until you understand the ceiling.

Unit economics template

Before any partnership discussion, model the following at 1%, 2% and 5% conversion.

Addressable audience size (realistic, not theoretical) Cost per introduction, co-marketing fee, or revenue-share rate Average order value and margin at point of sale Lifetime value if the relationship continues Breakeven volume and months to recover the cost of the test

Pillar 3 · Acquisitions

Buy momentum, not complexity.

A tuck-in is not a growth strategy. It is an accelerant for a growth strategy that already exists. The question is never "could we acquire this?" It is "does acquiring this make the thing we are already building faster or cheaper to build?"

If the answer requires the acquisition to succeed for the underlying strategy to work, the acquisition is plugging a hole, not adding fuel.

When a tuck-in makes sense

  • The target brings a customer relationship or distribution channel we would otherwise spend 18 to 24 months building.
  • The target brings a capability (technology, license, team) that is genuinely faster to buy than build.
  • The economics of the combined entity beat the sum of its parts at realistic integration cost.
  • Integration complexity is proportionate to the size of the business managing it.

When to walk away

  • The deal is driven by the seller's timeline or the attractiveness of the asset, not by strategic fit.
  • Integration requires a different operating model than the acquirer currently runs.
  • The financial case depends on synergies that have not been tested in practice.
  • There is no clear answer to: "who runs this the day after close?"

The RGP acquisition screen

  1. Strategic fit. Does this accelerate an existing pillar, or create a new dependency?
  2. Operational fit. Can the acquirer's team integrate this without a step-change in complexity?
  3. Financial case. What are the unit economics at base case, not best case?
  4. Integration plan. Who owns what on Day 1, Day 30, and Day 90?
  5. Exit hypothesis. What does success look like, and how long does it take to know?

We do not earn on transaction close. Our incentive is for the acquisition to make the client's business better, not for the deal to happen. If the screen does not pass, we say so.

How an engagement runs

1Growth auditUnderstand what is actually blocking growth
  • Map current revenue by channel, customer type, and margin.
  • Identify the two or three constraints that, if removed, would change the trajectory.
  • Audit existing marketing, sales, and CRM infrastructure.
  • Score the partner and acquisition landscape against the rubric.
  • Produce a written audit: where you are, what is blocking you, and the sequence.
2StructureBuild the shared language and operating infrastructure
  • Define the ideal customer profile (ICP) and the offer for each segment.
  • Build or rebuild the funnel: capture, nurture, conversion.
  • Stand up CRM scoring, routing, and SLA alerts.
  • Establish the weekly operating rhythm: what gets reviewed, who owns it, what triggers action.
3Test and iterateRun the first tests with discipline
  • Activate the first two to three channels or partnerships.
  • Hold to the test and pilot distinction: no pivots mid-flight.
  • Review weekly against the operating rhythm.
  • Document every result: what worked, what did not, and what the next test is.
4HandoffThe system is embedded and the client can run it
  • Document the full growth system: what is running, what the metrics mean, who owns what.
  • Train the internal team on the operating rhythm.
  • Define the trigger conditions for the next phase of work.
  • Produce a handoff brief: the state of the business, the open experiments, and the recommended next 90 days.

The weekly operating rhythm

Every active engagement runs on a weekly review. The format is the same regardless of phase.

What tests are live, and what is the current read? What was the result of anything that concluded? What is the one decision that needs to be made this week? What is on the horizon that we need to prepare for now?

Section 8

Best practices across the three pillars

We structure the work and teach the practices behind each pillar, so your team can repeat them without us.

  • Diffuse positioning is almost always the first thing to fix. One story, one funnel, one source of truth.
  • The ICP is not the customer you have; it is the customer for whom your economics work best at scale.
  • Clarity on the ICP changes everything downstream: the channel, the offer, the partnership criteria.
  • One question, one accountable owner.
  • Set the metric, budget and decision rule before launch, and never start a test without a written success condition.
  • Establish a baseline, so improvement is measurable, and never read a result without checking the volume first.
  • Never run two tests that share a variable. Record every result, including the failures.
  • The fastest way to waste a quarter is to call a pilot a test.
  • Score every candidate before any conversation. Enthusiasm from a partner is not a criterion.
  • Choose for shared values, complementary strengths and alignment. The right first partner makes the next conversation easier.
  • Put scope, duration, success measures and exit terms in writing, and stage commitments: start small and expand as results earn it.
  • Settle customer ownership, data, IP and liability early. Do not negotiate exclusivity before you understand the ceiling.
  • Name the conflicts. A partner who does not know about competing relationships is a partner you will lose.
  • Start with a thesis: why buy rather than build or partner.
  • Source through relationships and network, not only brokered processes.
  • Finance, price and structure to align interests and limit downside. The financial case should survive the base scenario, not just the upside.
  • The integration plan is part of the deal, not a post-close problem: an owner and a 100-day plan before close.
  • If you cannot name the person who runs this on Day 1, you are not ready to close.
  • Family businesses often wait too long to build external advisory benches. Build early.
  • The value of outside judgment is not expertise; it is permission to challenge instinct.
  • Vet advisors openly. Name the potential conflicts before they surface.

Quick reference

The growth question checklist

Before starting any engagement phase, answer these.

  • What is the next revenue question this work is trying to answer?
  • What is the one constraint that, if removed, changes the trajectory?
  • What are we testing, and what does a passing result look like?
  • Who is accountable for the outcome of this phase?
  • What does the handoff look like if this works?

Red flags

No shared definition of what "working" means.
Multiple variables being tested at once.
Partners evaluated on enthusiasm, not rubric.
Acquisition driven by seller timeline, not strategic fit.
Operating rhythm missing or not being respected.
Diffuse positioning tolerated because "our clients know who we are."
